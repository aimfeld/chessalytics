You're two pawns up.
Do you actually win?

In one of our recent exchanges, GM Noël Studer of Next Level Chess suggested looking into what actually becomes of a big engine advantage at the end of the opening. So I had a close look at games in the FlawChess benchmark database where a player, facing an opponent of nearly the same rating, reached the middlegame with a Stockfish evaluation of at least +2.0, the engine's way of saying "about two pawns up". That can mean extra material, a superior position, or both. Then I checked how those games actually ended for different rating levels and time controls. The answer might change how you think about "winning positions".

1

A two-pawn lead is not a won game, at any level

Players who start the middlegame two pawns up or better go on to lose roughly one game in four. And this is not just a beginner problem: even at 2400 Lichess, more than one of these games in five still ends in defeat for the player who had the advantage.

"Two pawns up" usually means more than two pawns

Two pawns is the floor of this group, not its typical member. Half of these leads are bigger than +3.6 pawns, and roughly one in four is +5 or more, worth about a rook. Size changes everything: a bare two-pawn lead is lost 32% of the time, while a five-pawn lead is lost only 14.6% of the time. The 25% headline is an average across all of them.

Masters hold their leads better. The averages hide it.

Split every one of these games into four endings and something odd appears: the share of sustained-lead wins (the advantage stays at two pawns or more the whole way from middlegame entry to the win) barely moves with rating, sitting near 40% everywhere. It is tempting to conclude that masters are no better at holding on to a lead. That conclusion is wrong, and the reason is section 3.

Two ways to lose a won game: outplayed, or flagged

At 800, 30% of two-pawn blitz leads end in defeat against 22% in classical, so blitz looks like the worst place to hold an advantage. But split those defeats by how the game actually ended and two very different problems appear. Losing on the board is a rating problem: the three time controls sit almost on top of each other, falling from roughly 20% at club level to 13–16% at 2400. Losing on the clock is a time-control problem: blitz leaders flag away 6–9% of these winning positions at every rating, rapid players about 3%, classical players about 1%, and getting stronger barely moves it. Those flag falls are genuine throwaways: in 41% of them the engine still had the blitz leader two pawns up on the final move.

Games LOST by the leader: on the board vs on the clock

Takeaway: nearly one in three blitz leads that get thrown away are thrown away on the clock, not on the board. Improving your chess fixes board losses, but it barely touches flagging. If you keep losing won blitz games, part of the cure is not tactics at all, it's the clock: moving from blitz to rapid cuts flag losses from about 7% to 3% of these winning positions, and classical nearly eliminates them. Only above 2000 does speed also start costing games on the board: at 2400, with clock-decided games removed from the count entirely, blitz leaders still lose 19% of these positions against 14% in classical.

What to take to your next game

  • A lead is a promise, not a result. Players who reach the middlegame two pawns up still lose one game in four, and at exactly two pawns the risk is closer to one in three. Keep playing as if the game were undecided, because it is.
  • Holding a lead is its own skill. Given the same size of lead at middlegame entry, sustained-lead wins (the lead never dips below two pawns for the rest of the game) climb steadily with rating: 16% at 800 to 23% at 2400 for a two-to-three-pawn entry lead. Winning the material was only half the work; the other half is the unglamorous part, trading down and shutting off counterplay.
  • Losing won blitz games? The fix may be a slower time control, not more tactics. Blitz leaders flag away 6–9% of these games at every rating, and getting stronger barely helps. Playing rapid instead cuts that to about 3%, and classical nearly eliminates it.

About this analysis

I analyzed 329,518 Lichess games (blitz, rapid and classical) with full Stockfish evaluations, across five rating groups from 800 to 2400, keeping only games between opponents within 100 rating points of each other (460,604 games before that matching filter; the first version of this story skipped it, which flattered the 2400 numbers by a few points). "Two pawns up" means the engine evaluation reached ≥ 200 centipawns for one side at the first move of the middlegame. Where the middlegame starts is decided by Lichess's own phase rules (the same division you see on its analysis board, based on piece counts, back-rank development, and how entangled the two sides' pieces are). Full methodology, exact queries, and every table are in the technical report. GM Noël Studer suggested the question; the analysis, and any errors in it, are mine.

Read the technical report · Browse the benchmark database

Data: Lichess games with server-side Stockfish analysis, which players request themselves, so these are not a uniform sample of Lichess; analyzed games also lean toward the games their players wanted examined, often losses, so the absolute rates carry some selection tilt (the technical report quantifies it). Ratings are Lichess ratings at the time each game was played, grouped into 400-point bands, and only games where the two players were within 100 rating points of each other are counted. This is an engine evaluation, not a material count: a position can be worth +2.0 with the material dead level. "Two pawns up" is a floor, not a description: the median lead in this group is +3.6 pawns, which is why section 3 breaks the results out by lead size. Section 5 splits losses by Lichess's own game-termination field; "abandoned" (a disconnect) is counted separately from a clock forfeit. Unrated games and games against a bot are excluded. Small samples: 800 and 2400 classical have the fewest games, so treat those two cells as approximate.
