You're two pawns up.
Do you actually win?

We took every game in our benchmark database where a player reached the middlegame at least two pawns up by Stockfish's count, and checked how those games actually ended. The answer should change how you think about "winning positions".

All 112,583 games that entered the middlegame with a ≥ two-pawn engine advantage, all ratings combined. "At least" matters: the median lead here is +3.6 pawns, and a bare two-pawn lead does considerably worse than the headline (section 3).

1

A two-pawn lead is not a won game, at any level

Players who start the middlegame two pawns up or better go on to lose roughly one game in four. And this is not just a beginner problem: even at 2400 Lichess, nearly one of these games in five still ends in defeat for the player who had the advantage.

Takeaway: the engine bar says "winning". The scoreboard says "maybe". A lead is a promise you still have to keep.

2

Big leads are everywhere at club level, rare among masters

At 800, a majority of games (58%) already have a two-pawn advantage for someone when the middlegame starts: openings decide games early. By 2400 that collapses to 12%. Strong players simply don't hand over (or receive) free material in the opening.

3

"Two pawns up" usually means more than two pawns

Two pawns is the floor of this group, not its typical member. Half of these leads are bigger than +3.6 pawns, and roughly one in four is +5 or more, which is a whole rook. Size changes everything: a bare two-pawn lead is lost 30% of the time, while a five-pawn lead is lost only 13.6% of the time. The 24% headline is an average across all of them.

Games LOST by the leader, by how big the lead was when the middlegame began

Takeaway: if you are exactly two pawns up, your real risk is closer to one loss in three than one in four. Every number elsewhere on this page is flattered by the games where somebody was already a piece ahead.

4

Masters hold their leads better. The averages hide it.

Split every one of these games into four endings and something odd appears: the share of sustained-lead wins (the advantage stays at two pawns or more the whole way from middlegame entry to the win) barely moves with rating, sitting near 43% everywhere. It is tempting to conclude that masters are no better at holding on to a lead. That conclusion is wrong, and the reason is section 3.

Weaker players are handed much bigger leads (median +4.5 pawns at 800 against +3.0 at 2400), and bigger leads are easier to hold on to. That mix effect exactly cancels the skill effect and flattens the green bar. Compare like with like, the same size of lead at middlegame entry for every rating, and the flatness disappears.

Takeaway: masters are better at holding a lead and better at fighting back when it slips. Given the same two-to-three-pawn lead, sustained wins climb from 17% at 800 to 27% at 2400. The flat bar in the first chart is an illusion produced by who gets which size of lead.

5

Two ways to lose a won game: outplayed, or flagged

At 800, 29% of two-pawn blitz leads end in defeat against 22% in classical, so blitz looks like the worst place to hold an advantage. But split those defeats by how the game actually ended and two very different problems appear. Losing on the board is a rating problem: the three time controls sit almost on top of each other, falling from roughly 20% at club level to 11–14% at 2400. Losing on the clock is a time-control problem: blitz leaders flag away 7–9% of these won games at every rating, rapid players about 2.5%, classical players about 1%, and getting stronger barely moves it. Those flag falls are genuine throwaways: in 41% of them the engine still had the blitz leader two pawns up on the final move.

Games LOST by the leader: on the board vs on the clock

Takeaway: improving your chess fixes board losses, but it barely touches flagging. If you keep losing won blitz games, part of the cure is not tactics at all, it's the clock: moving from blitz to rapid cuts flag losses from about 7% to 2.5% of won games, and classical nearly eliminates them. Only above 2000 does speed also start costing games on the board: at 2400, blitz board losses are 14.4% against 10.6% in classical.

About this analysis

We analyzed 460,604 Lichess games (blitz, rapid and classical) with full Stockfish evaluations, across five rating groups from 800 to 2400. "Two pawns up" means the engine evaluation reached ≥ 200 centipawns for one side at the first move of the middlegame. Where the middlegame starts is decided by Lichess's own phase rules (the same division you see on its analysis board, based on piece counts, back-rank development, and how entangled the two sides' pieces are). Full methodology, exact queries, and every table are in the technical report.

Read the technical report · Browse the benchmark database

Data: Lichess games with server-side Stockfish analysis, which players request themselves, so these are not a uniform sample of Lichess. Ratings are Lichess ratings at the time each game was played, grouped into 400-point bands, and opponents are rating-matched. "Two pawns up" is a floor, not a description: the median lead in this group is +3.6 pawns, which is why section 3 breaks the results out by lead size. Section 5 splits losses by Lichess's own game-termination field; "abandoned" (a disconnect) is counted separately from a clock forfeit. Unrated games and games against a bot are excluded. Small samples: 800 and 2400 classical have the fewest games, so treat those two cells as approximate.
