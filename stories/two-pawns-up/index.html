A two-pawn lead is not a won game, at any level

Players who start the middlegame two pawns up go on to lose roughly one game in four. And this is not just a beginner problem: even at master level (2400 Lichess), nearly one of these games in five still ends in defeat for the player who had the advantage.

Takeaway: the engine bar says "winning". The scoreboard says "maybe". A lead is a promise you still have to keep.

Big leads are everywhere at club level, rare among masters

At 800, a majority of games (58%) already have a two-pawn advantage for someone when the middlegame starts: openings decide games early. By 2400 that collapses to 12%. Strong players simply don't hand over (or receive) free material in the opening.

3

What rating actually buys you

Split every one of these games into four endings and something surprising appears: the share of sustained-lead wins (the lead never dips below two pawns and converts) barely moves with rating: about 43% everywhere. What improves with strength is everything else: winning anyway after the lead slips, and above all, not losing outright.

Four outcomes of a two-pawn middlegame lead

Takeaway: masters aren't better at cruising; they're better at fighting when the cruise fails.

4

Want to keep your lead? Slow down.

The same advantage survives very differently depending on the clock. At every rating, leads die most often in blitz and least often in classical. The gap is brutal at the top: a 2400 loses a two-pawn lead almost twice as often in blitz (22%) as in classical (12%).

Games LOST by the leader, from a two-pawn middlegame lead

Takeaway: a 2400 in blitz throws away big leads about as often as an 800 in classical (both ≈ 21%). Time, not just talent, converts advantages.

5

The biggest threat to your lead? You.

So who actually throws leads away? Up to the moment the lead exists, the blunders are mostly your opponent's: that's how leads are born. From that moment on, the picture flips. Counting only blunders made after the middlegame starts two pawns up, the leader blunders more than the opponent, about 1.2× more, at every single rating.

Blunders per game after the middlegame starts with a two-pawn lead

Takeaway: once you're ahead, your opponent is no longer the main source of danger. You are. Winning positions don't defend themselves; they have to be played as carefully as bad ones.

About this analysis

We analyzed 478,340 Lichess games (blitz, rapid and classical) with full Stockfish evaluations, across five rating groups from 800 to 2400. "Two pawns up" means the engine evaluation reached ≥ 200 centipawns for one side at the first move of the middlegame. Full methodology, exact queries and every table are in the technical report.

Read the technical report

Data: Lichess games with server-side Stockfish analysis; ratings are Lichess ratings at the time each game was played. Blunder counts use Lichess's own definition (a move that loses at least 15 percentage points of winning chances), recomputed per move from the stored evaluations. Small samples: 800 and 2400 classical have the fewest games, so treat those two cells as approximate.
